01 · Foundation

Why layout, not equipment, decides the outcome

Automatic insertion is a tolerance-stacking problem. Five independent error sources must sum to less than the clearance available. If any one of them consumes more than its share, no machine accuracy can recover the result.

Error source 1–2

Machine and holder

Machine positioning repeatability plus workboard holder accuracy. The holder is a design consequence — standardise datum holes and the holder becomes a family asset rather than a per-product tool.

Error source 3–4

Board pattern and leads

Where the holes actually are versus where the CAD file says, plus component lead straightness and tip configuration. Fabrication tolerance is spent before the board reaches the line.

Error source 5

Hole diameter and position

Insertion holes hold ±0.07 mm position tolerance; datum holes hold ±0.05 mm. Both are tighter than most designers assume.

Three inputs set the economics of THT assembly: the circuit boards, the equipment, and the components to be inserted. Standardising all three reduces process variability, which raises insertion reliability and lowers production cost. The boards are the only one you fully control at the design stage — which is why the design guideline conversation has to happen before the purchase order.

02 · Board Considerations

Size, shape, panelisation, thickness, warpage

These five are decided at fabrication and are rarely reviewed afterwards. They determine whether the board can be handled at all, before any component question is asked.

Shape

Standalone machines place boards on a dedicated workboard holder, so rectangular or square shape is not critical. Machines with automatic board handling need symmetry, controlled warpage and no cutouts in the clamp zone. The preferred configuration uses breakaway inserts to yield a symmetrical panel with a consistent process edge.

Panelisation

Positioning small boards into multiple breakaway arrays raises throughput by amortising handling overhead across more units per transfer. Standardising panel size across a product family removes changeover time, because the same holder and handling setup apply to every board in the family.

Warpage

Warpage causes transfer errors and degrades insertion performance. Note the asymmetry: radial insertion allows only 1.60 mm on one axis, while axial and DIP allow 3.17 mm on both. A board that passes axial may still fail radial.

02.1 · Location References

Datum holes and axis objectives

Datum holes are the locating references every other hole is drilled relative to, and the features the workboard holder clamps on. Standardising them is the single cheapest changeover reduction available.

FeatureMinRecommendedMax
Primary datum hole D13.17 mm3.96 mm6.35 mm
Secondary datum hole D23.17 mm—6.35 mm
Pass-Thru II handling——4.74 mm
Insertion hole position tol.±0.07 mm (0.003")
Datum hole position tol.±0.05 mm (0.002")

Four rules

  1. Position datum holes with the longest lateral span between them — angular error scales with span.
  2. Standardise datum diameter and spacing across the product family. This is the changeover lever, and it is free.
  3. On Pass-Thru II board handling, the locating hole maximum is 4.74 mm. A 6.35 mm datum hole is valid standalone and out of spec here.
  4. Hold datum position tolerance at ±0.05 mm. Insertion holes get ±0.07 mm; datums are tighter because everything references them.

Axis objectives

Insertion is possible at 0° and 90° only. On DIP and axial inserters, components should be inserted in one axis only for maximum throughput. Radial machines insert at 0° or 90° without affecting throughput. Two-axis insertion is acceptable and efficient; other angles are not possible.

03 · Workboard Holders

The interface between your datum design and the machine

A holder that does not locate the board accurately and repeatably will reduce insertion performance regardless of machine capability. Choose by lot size and changeover frequency.

Large lots · medium-to-high changeover

Custom holder

Designed for specific board sizes, giving the highest positional accuracy. Multiple boards can share one custom holder if sizes and locating holes are standardised — which is exactly why datum standardisation pays.

Prototype · contract manufacturing

Adjustable holder

Accommodates multiple board sizes with low-to-medium changeover. The usual choice for high-mix environments. Note that an adjustable holder cannot compensate for datum holes that move between products — it only accommodates boards that are individually correct.

High volume · low-to-medium changeover

Automatic handling

BHS (Board Handling System) supports left-to-right or right-to-left transfer on single or dual head machines. SBT (Single Board Transfer) suits same-side in/out layouts on single head machines but narrows the thickness range to 1.52–2.36 mm.

04 · Axial Lead Component Insertion

Hole span is the formula almost nobody applies

Axial insertion damages component bodies for one dominant reason: the hole span is smaller than the body requires. The driver tips close on the body because there is nowhere for it to go.

Minimum insertion hole span

Hole span is the distance between hole centres. Design the span greater than the calculated minimum, never equal to it — body lengths vary between production lots without a part-number change.

// Metric formulas Standard tooling Min span = [(Body length × 1.112) + 2.36 mm] − Lead dia Large lead tooling Min span = [(Body length × 1.085) + 4.11 mm] − Lead dia 5 mm tooling Min span = [(Body length × 1.109) + 1.40 mm] − Lead dia 5.5 mm tooling Min span = [(Body length × 1.067) + 2.30 mm] − Lead dia // Non-symmetrical components: subtract a further 0.41 mm (0.016") // from the maximum body length before applying the formula.

Maximum component body diameter

In axial insertion the overall form length is fixed, so board thickness and body diameter compete for the same space.

Max body dia = Fixed form length − (2 × board thickness) Standard / high density / large lead tooling form length 10.69 mm (0.420") 5 mm / 5.5 mm tooling form length 11.68 mm (0.460") // Example: 1.57 mm board, standard tooling Max body dia = 10.69 − (2 × 1.57) = 7.55 mm

The 5 mm trap

At a 5 mm insertion span, maximum component body diameter collapses to 2.29 mm and maximum lead diameter to 0.61 mm. This is a genuinely tight window and the origin of most "the machine cannot handle our parts" complaints.

Span stability limits

On the 6295, 6292 and 6287 series, insertion span across five consecutive insertions should not vary by more than 10 mm. On the 6241, the limit is 5 mm across two consecutive insertions. Boards mixing a 7.5 mm resistor and a 22 mm wire link in adjacent positions can trip this.

Lead hole diameter

Hole diameter = lead diameter + 0.48 mm ± 0.08 mm. Too small degrades insertion reliability; too large leaves loose components in the board and an unreliable clinch.

Clinch lengths and angles

Clinch angle is adjustable across 0° to 45°.
Clinch lead length is adjustable from 1.28 mm (0.050") to 1.80 mm (0.071"), measured from the centre of the insertion hole to the end of the lead.

Both are tooling parameters — changing either requires a tooling change, not a software setting. The achieved angle may vary depending on lead hardness and hole diameter, so the specified figure is a target, not a guarantee.

Topside clearances

Outside formers guide the leads to the point of insertion, so clearance around each hole must allow the tooling to function:

  • C = 0.25 mm (0.010") — recommended for all tooling styles
  • D = 0.76 mm (0.030") — recommended for all tooling styles

05 · Radial Component Insertion

Pitch is the constraint, and it is set by purchasing

Radial insertion covers the largest share of through-hole components on consumer, lighting and power boards. The design rules are simple; the failures come from component supply drifting away from the layout.

Recommended lead hole span by component pitch

Component pitchRecommended lead hole spanNote
2.5 mm components2.54 mm (0.100")Fixed span for lead diameters up to 0.86 mm
5.0 mm componentsLead diameter + 4.5 mm (0.177")0.36 mm lead → 4.85 mm span
7.5 mm componentsLead diameter + 7.04 mm (0.277")0.36 mm lead → 7.39 mm span
10.0 mm componentsLead diameter + 9.58 mm (0.377")0.36 mm lead → 9.93 mm span

Lead hole diameter

Hole diameter = maximum lead diameter + 0.48 mm ± 0.08 mm.

Unguided leads — the third leg of a triangular layout, in-line SIP and potentiometer leads — need a larger clearance because nothing holds them on centre: lead diameter + 0.58 mm ± 0.08 mm.

Component weight limit

The Radial 8 inserts components weighing up to 5 grams. Mass and centre of gravity both matter: a short 5 g component with its centre of gravity 20.32 mm (0.80") above the feed hole may run, while a tall 5 g component with a higher centre of gravity may not.

Reel and tape limits

Maximum reel weight 1.8 kg (4 lbs) — beyond that, consult the factory.

Taped components must unwind from reel and cassette, or unfold from ammo-pack, with a force not exceeding 5 Newtons (17.6 oz).

Note on lead hole spans. These dimensions apply only to holes for leads captured by the insertion tooling — the jaw clamps secure the component leads against the fixed surfaces of the jaw guide. All remaining holes should be drilled according to the component manufacturer's specified spacing requirements.

06 · Dual In-Line Package Insertion

The diagonal is the diameter that matters

DIP hole sizing has its own formula because a rectangular lead's effective diameter is its diagonal, not its width. Calculating from the width alone is the classic DIP hole sizing error.

// Minimum hole diameter for reliable DIP insertion Min hole dia = Max lead dia + Hole location tolerance + 0.25 mm (0.010") // Max lead diameter is the lead cross-section diagonal C = √(A² + B²) A and B = lead cross-section dimensions // Example: 0.64 mm × 0.38 mm rectangular lead C = √(0.64² + 0.38²) = 0.74 mm // Not 0.64 mm — the width alone understates the requirement

Lead tip configuration

  • Tapered lead tip — preferred. Self-centres against the hole.
  • Squared lead tip — acceptable. Positional tolerance has to absorb the full misalignment.
  • Tip radius: 0.36 mm (0.015") maximum
  • Lead dimensions: 1.78 mm max / 0.76 mm min (long axis); 1.17 mm max / 0.38 mm min (short axis)

Clinch lengths and angles

ParameterDIP moduleDIP socket
Inward clinch angle20° +8°0° +10° / −0°
Outward clinchAvailableNot recommended
Minimum dimension A / B1.02 mm (0.040")1.02 mm (0.040")
Clinch length tolerance0.64 ± 0.13 mm0.64 ± 0.13 mm

Outward clinching is explicitly not recommended for DIP socket module insertion. Tooling change is required to change lead clinch angle or length. Angle may vary depending on lead hardness and hole diameter.

Machined pin DIP sockets

A tapered bushing is recommended for auto insertion. A non-tapered bushing contributes directly to reduced insertion reliability — the lead catches on the aperture edge and the head mis-seats. This is a purchasing decision, not a process one: request the tapered variant at quotation.

06.1 · Mixed Technology

DIP tooling clearances against adjacent SMT

On mixed-technology boards, the insertion and clinch tooling must clear SMT components on both sides. Three clearances need checking: DIP tooling to SMT on the topside, socket tooling to SMT on the topside, and clinch tooling to SMT on the bottomside. The bottomside clinch anvil is the most commonly overlooked — the board looks clear from above and the anvil collides with a neighbouring joint from below.

07 · Line Configuration

From design rules to station planning

A real board never maps to a single machine. The design guideline output is a station plan — and an honest count of the operations that stay manual.

1

Radial platform — taped, standard pitch

The majority of component count and the source of the throughput figure. A 2.5 / 5.0 / 7.5 / 10.0 mm tooling envelope covers every pitch on a mixed board family without a tooling changeover — which is what makes a shared family viable on one setup.

2

Odd-form platform — connectors, transformers, terminals

Gripper tooling with tray, bowl and tube feeding. Lower cycle rate, higher component variety. Reel-fed terminals belong here too, where clinch geometry determines the mechanical joint quality rather than just retention.

3

Manual station — defined, not residual

Components with no handling surface or no taped supply stay manual. The point of the evaluation is to name them and count them, so headcount planning is based on a number rather than an assumption.

4

Board handling and inline transfer

BHS for left-to-right or right-to-left transfer on single or dual head machines; SBT for same-side in/out layouts. Check thickness against the handling choice — SBT narrows the range to 1.52–2.36 mm.

Service commitment

7×24 worldwide support · Free installation and training · 1 day lead time on spare parts · 1 month customised solution turnaround.

Feeders, designed and manufactured in-house

Tape, tray, tube, bowl, belt and label feeders — plus custom THT feeders built around your specific component. We design and manufacture the feeder, nozzle and gripper tooling rather than reselling it.

Compatibility

Nozzles and feeders are supplied for Panasonic, Yamaha, Fuji, JUKI, Samsung, Hitachi, ASM, Siemens, Sanyo, Mirae, Casio, Assembleon, Sony and Hanwha platforms. A factory already running another brand is the most natural starting point.

The station plan

Station 1

Radial platform

All taped, standard-pitch components. A 2.5 / 5.0 / 7.5 / 10.0 mm tooling envelope covers every pitch on both panels without a tooling changeover — the reason the shared family works on one setup. Source of the 940 UPH figure.

Station 2

Odd-form platform

USB-A connector and transformer, both tray-supplied, both requiring gripper tooling rather than radial insertion. Verify insertion force for the transformer during evaluation rather than commissioning.

Station 3

Manual station — defined

The fusible resistor with heat-shrink sleeving has no handling surface, and the transistor is supplied in stick format at 2.3 mm pitch with no taped equivalent. Two operations, counted, not residual.

The verdict table is worth more than the quotation. It converts an open-ended automation ambition into a specific scope: this many components on this platform, this many on that platform, this many remain manual, this is the headcount. A line designed for the real component set hits its numbers. A line designed for an optimistic component set does not — and the shortfall is usually blamed on the equipment.
